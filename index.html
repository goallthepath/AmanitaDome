Build guide

Press Build it above the dome: the finished frame swirls away, the view moves in, and the dome goes up in the order listed here. With struts & hubs the struts rise one after another, row by row from the base. With triangle frames every triangle is first framed and covered on its own, spread out around the sphere, and the finished triangles are then drawn together. That takes about twice the timber (every inner edge has two struts side by side), but each panel can be covered on the bench.

    Panel drawings

    Every distinct triangle drawn once, to scale, with its sides lettered and measured hub to hub, its corner angles and its area. With triangle frames the timber band is drawn inside the edges at the section you set.

    Strut drawings

    One sheet per strut type, drawn as a timber piece seen from the side (the depth face, where the end cuts show), with break lines because the piece is far longer than it is deep. The end angle is measured from square and assumes a flat hub plate sitting square to the sphere's radius.

    A few notes before you cut anything

    Hub-to-hub lengths are chord factor × radius. The cut length has your hub allowance taken off both ends, so it's the number to mark on the timber or tube.

    Class I is the usual triangle grid drawn parallel to the solid's edges; class II turns that grid by 30° (it's built by first splitting every face into three around its centre, so it only comes in even frequencies). Equal chords places the grid points on the flat face and pushes them out to the sphere; equal arcs spaces them evenly along the arcs instead, which usually evens out the strut lengths a little.

    The cut always follows a row of hubs. With a vertex on top and an odd frequency (3V, 5V) that row isn't perfectly level, which is why the classic 3V 5/8 dome needs packing under a few feet. The base unevenness figure tells you how much. With an edge or a face on top the rows are rarely level at all, so treat the cut there as a starting point.

    The end angle is asin(chord factor / 2), the angle between the strut and the hub's tangent plane. Cut both ends at that angle, measured from square, and the outer face comes out longer than the inner face by depth × tan(angle). If you use a pipe-and-hub system instead of flat plates, the same angle is the one you flatten and bend the pipe ends to.

    The bevel is the angle to plane off the long edges of a strut so two neighbouring panels lie flat on it: half the fold between those panels. It varies a little within a strut type, so the table shows the mean.

    Triangle frames: every triangle gets its own three struts, so an inner edge carries two struts side by side and the cut list counts both. The struts are inset by half their width so the pairs sit flush; corners are mitred in the plane of the triangle rather than cut for a hub.

    For reference: an icosahedral class I 2V half dome comes out at 65 struts, a 3V 5/8 at 165, a 4V half at 250.
